AI Patient Support Assistant — Case Study

Overview

A conversational AI assistant that helps patients get specialty medications by explaining insurance, prior authorization, copay assistance and enrollment in plain language, and handing off to a person when needed.

Industry Pharmaceutical / Healthcare
Focus Conversational and agentic AI, UX research, patient experience
Role UX Researcher and Conversation Designer (solo)
Project type Self-initiated concept project
Status Concept, prototype and research plan complete; participant research is the next phase

Problem

Patients prescribed specialty medications can wait days or weeks to start treatment, because access depends on several separate steps that no one explains end to end.

  • Insurance verification — is the drug covered, and at what tier?
  • Prior authorization — the prescriber must justify the drug to the insurer, often without the patient knowing.
  • Financial assistance — copay cards and foundations each have their own eligibility rules.
  • Enrollment and specialty pharmacy — forms, documents and calls across several organizations.

Design question: can a conversational AI assistant help patients understand these steps and know their next action — without giving medical advice, overstating certainty, or hiding the path to a human?

My role

I owned the project end to end as the researcher and conversation designer.

  1. Framed the problem and the design question.
  2. Mapped the patient journey from prescription to first refill.
  3. Designed six assistant scenarios, guardrails and human hand-off rules.
  4. Built a testable conversational prototype.
  5. Wrote the research plan, consent script, interview guide and usability tasks.
  6. Built the analysis workflow: a session workbook and a Python theme-coding script.

Patient journey

The journey has six stages; prior authorization and financial assistance are where confusion is expected to peak. These are working hypotheses that the research phase will test.

Stage Patient's question Likely pain point Where AI could help
1. Prescription received "What happens now?" No one explains the steps ahead Plain-language overview of the road to first dose
2. Insurance check "Is this covered?" Coverage terms are confusing Explain tiers, deductibles, what to ask the insurer
3. Prior authorization "Why is it delayed?" Invisible process, no status Explain PA, typical documents, who does what
4. Financial assistance "Can I afford it?" Many programs, unclear eligibility Narrow down programs and eligibility questions
5. Enrollment "What forms do I need?" Paperwork across organizations Document checklist, review before submit
6. First fill and refills "When does it arrive?" Hard-to-follow pharmacy calls Status explanation and refill reminders

Assistant design

The assistant explains processes and next steps; it never diagnoses, prescribes, or promises coverage.

Scenarios: insurance and coverage guidance · prior authorization explanation · copay and financial assistance · enrollment walkthrough · application status · hand-off to a human.

Key design decisions

Decision Rationale
Administrative guidance only, no medical advice Medical questions need a clinician; mixing the two would put patients at risk and blur accountability
State uncertainty plainly ("Only your insurer can confirm this") A confident wrong answer about coverage costs patients money and trust
Ask for the minimum personal data, and say why first Health and insurance data is sensitive; explaining purpose makes sharing a choice
Summary and explicit confirmation before any submission Patients stay in control of consequential actions
"Talk to a person" available at every step AI should reduce effort, not become a barrier to help

Hand off to a human when the patient asks a medical question, is denied coverage, shows distress or can't afford treatment, or asks the same thing twice.

Prototype

The prototype is a configured LLM conversation (ChatGPT) that behaves as the assistant, so the concept can be tested with real people before any engineering. It uses a fictional drug ("Veltrana") and fictional programs so no one mistakes it for a real product.

You are "CareBridge", a patient support assistant for a fictional specialty medication called Veltrana (made by the fictional company Northwind Pharma).

Your job: help patients understand medication access steps — insurance coverage, prior authorization, copay assistance, enrollment, and refill status.

Rules:
1. Use plain language at about a 6th–8th grade reading level. Short answers, then offer more detail.
2. Never give medical advice (dosage, side effects, whether to take or stop the drug). Say: "That's a question for your doctor or pharmacist. Want me to connect you with a nurse line?"
3. Never promise coverage or eligibility. Say what is uncertain and who can confirm it.
4. Ask only for the information you need, and say why before asking.
5. Before anything is "submitted", show a summary and ask the patient to confirm.
6. Offer "talk to a person" whenever the patient seems confused, upset, is denied coverage, or asks the same thing twice.
7. Fictional facts you can use: The Northwind Copay Card lowers copays to as little as $10/month for commercially insured patients (not Medicare/Medicaid). Prior authorization usually takes 3–10 business days. Enrollment needs: insurance card, prescription details, and a signed consent form.

Start by greeting the patient and asking how you can help today.

Research approach

Five 30–40 minute moderated sessions, each combining a short discovery interview with a usability test of the prototype. Participants are adults who have handled insurance for a prescription, or caregivers who did so for a family member.

Interview themes: past access experiences, the most confusing step, cost barriers, attitudes to AI help, and what would build or break trust.

Usability tasks

Task Scenario Success looks like
T1 Coverage Find out what happens next with insurance States the next step in their own words
T2 Prior authorization Learn what PA means and how long it takes Explains PA and the 3–10 day range
T3 Cost help Look for help with a $400 copay Finds the copay card and notices its eligibility limits
T4 Medical question Ask about a drug interaction Assistant declines and offers a human; participant notices
T5 Get a person Ask to talk to someone Reaches a hand-off in two messages or fewer

Evaluation framework: task completion, time, errors, and a 1–7 confidence rating per task, reviewed across five dimensions — trust, transparency, privacy, control, human support.

Ethics: informed consent, no real health or insurance details, anonymous IDs (P1–P5), recordings deleted after notes are written.
