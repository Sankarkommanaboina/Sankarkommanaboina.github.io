Agentic Commerce Assistant — UX Research Case Study

Overview

A research program for an AI assistant that helps bank customers discover, compare and apply for financial products — and, with permission, act on their behalf. The program asks when customers will trust an agent to recommend, prepare and execute financial tasks, and what the product must do to earn that trust responsibly.

Domain Financial services — agentic commerce
Product concept "Atlas", an AI assistant at the fictional Harbor Bank
Role UX Researcher (solo): strategy, methods, frameworks, prototype, analysis tooling
Methods Interviews, diary study, survey, concept testing, moderated usability, longitudinal benchmarking
Project type Self-initiated concept project
Status Research strategy, frameworks, instruments and prototype complete; fieldwork is the next phase

Problem and research questions

AI agents can now compare products, fill in applications and complete transactions — but in finance, a wrong or unexplained action costs customers money and trust. The product question is not "can the agent do it?" but "what will customers let it do, and under what conditions?"

# Research question Decision it informs
RQ1 How do customers express financial needs and goals to an AI assistant versus search or a banker? Intent model and conversation design
RQ2 Which tasks are customers willing to delegate, at what level of autonomy, and why? Autonomy defaults and roadmap sequencing
RQ3 Do customers understand AI recommendations well enough to make a confident, informed choice? Explanation and disclosure design
RQ4 What drives and blocks adoption — and which risks matter most to customers, Compliance and Risk? Launch criteria and safeguards
RQ5 How does trust change after repeated use, errors and recovery? Long-term success metrics

Delegation spectrum

Trust is not a single yes or no: customers are likely to accept AI at low autonomy and hesitate as it moves toward acting. I framed the agent's capabilities as five levels so research can measure willingness to delegate at each one.

Level The agent… Example What research must learn
L0 Inform Explains concepts and options "What's a balance transfer?" Is it accurate, clear and neutral?
L1 Recommend Ranks options for the customer "Best card for travel rewards" Is the rationale understood and believed?
L2 Prepare Pre-fills an application for review Drafts a card application Do customers review it, and catch errors?
L3 Execute with approval Acts after explicit confirmation Submits after "Yes, apply" Is consent informed and easy to refuse?
L4 Execute within limits Acts on standing rules Moves savings when balance > $X What limits, alerts and undo are required?

The survey measures comfort at each level, and usability sessions observe behavior at L1–L3 — comparing what people say with what they do.

Consumer intent framework

Customers rarely start with a product name; they start with a life event or goal. The framework links what customers say to the shopping mission behind it and the products that serve it, so the agent can respond to real intent rather than keywords.

Life event or goal How customers might say it Shopping mission Products and benefits
Moving out / first home "I need to build credit before renting" Build credit Secured card, credit-builder tools
Big trip coming up "Which card won't charge me abroad?" Optimize a purchase Travel card, no foreign transaction fee
Carrying a balance "I'm paying too much interest" Reduce cost of debt Balance transfer card, personal loan
New baby / job change "I need to start saving properly" Build a safety net High-yield savings, automatic transfers
Large purchase "Can I spread the cost of a laptop?" Finance a purchase Intro 0% APR card, installment plan

Research validates this framework: interviews and diary entries supply real phrasing, and card sorting checks that missions match how customers group their needs. It is a hypothesis until then.

Decision journey

The journey runs from intent to ongoing management; trust is most at risk at Apply, where the agent moves from advising to acting.

Stage Customer is asking Agent's role Research lens
1. Intent "What do I need?" Clarify the goal with a few questions How needs are expressed (RQ1)
2. Explore "What are my options?" Inform (L0) Comprehension, neutrality
3. Evaluate "Which is best for me, and why?" Recommend with rationale (L1) Explainability, calibrated trust
4. Consider "What's the catch?" Surface fees, terms, uncertainty Transparency, confidence
5. Apply "Do it for me?" Prepare (L2), then act with approval (L3) Control, consent, privacy
6. Manage "Is it working? Can I undo it?" Monitor and alert; act within limits (L4) Recourse, long-term trust

Research roadmap

A 12-week, four-phase plan moves from understanding needs to validating the concept to setting benchmarks, so each phase informs a specific product decision.

Phase Weeks Methods and sample Answers Decision it informs
1. Discover 1–4 12 in-depth interviews; 2-week diary study with 10 participants logging money decisions RQ1, RQ4 Opportunity areas and intent framework
2. Size 4–6 Online survey, n ≈ 400, with delegation scale per autonomy level RQ2, RQ4 Which segments and tasks to prioritize
3. Validate 6–10 Concept test of 3 autonomy variants; moderated usability with 8 participants on the prototype RQ2, RQ3 Autonomy defaults, explanation and consent design
4. Measure 10–12, then ongoing Baseline benchmark; longitudinal check-ins with pilot users at 30 and 90 days RQ5 Launch criteria and success metrics

Recruiting: a mix of financial confidence, digital comfort, age and income, including people who distrust AI — adoption barriers are as important as enthusiasm.

Trust and responsible AI framework

Every concept and prototype round is reviewed against seven dimensions, each with a question, the evidence that answers it, and the design safeguard it points to.

Dimension Review question Evidence Design safeguard
Trust calibration Do customers trust the agent as much as it deserves — no more, no less? Confidence vs. accuracy on tasks Show confidence and limits; never overstate
Transparency Do customers know why an option was recommended? Teach-back of the rationale Show key factors and trade-offs, not just a rank
Explainability Can customers inspect the evidence behind it? Source inspection, recall Show terms, fees, source and date in context
Control Can customers edit, pause, cancel or override? Task observation Persistent controls, approval gates, undo
Privacy Do customers understand what data is used and why? Permission comprehension Data minimization; just-in-time explanation
Fairness Could the experience disadvantage any group? Segmented results; expert review Monitor outcomes; accessible alternatives
Recourse Can customers get help or fix a mistake? Error-recovery tasks Human hand-off; clear dispute path

Findings are rated 1–5 per dimension and reviewed with Compliance, Risk and Legal before each release decision.

Benchmarks and success metrics

Success is measured across four layers, so the product is judged on whether customers understand and trust it appropriately — not only on conversions.

Layer Metric How measured
Usability Task success rate; time on task; UMUX-Lite Moderated sessions; post-task survey
Comprehension Teach-back accuracy on fees and terms Customer restates the recommendation in their own words
Trust Calibrated trust (confidence vs. correctness); willingness to delegate per level (1–7) Post-task rating; survey delegation scale
Control and safety Review rate before submit; errors caught; escalation rate to a human Prototype logs and observation
Business Application start-to-complete rate; drop-off by stage; 90-day retention of agent use Pilot analytics with Data Science

The first study sets the baseline; later rounds are compared against it.

Prototype and concept test

The prototype is a configured LLM conversation (ChatGPT) that behaves as Atlas, so autonomy levels and explanations can be tested with real people before engineering builds anything. Products and terms are fictional.

You are "Atlas", an AI assistant for the fictional Harbor Bank. You help customers find, compare and apply for Harbor Bank credit cards.

Fictional products:
- Harbor Everyday: no annual fee, 1.5% cash back, 22.9% APR.
- Harbor Voyager: $95 annual fee, 3x points on travel, no foreign transaction fees, 24.9% APR.
- Harbor Reset: no annual fee, 0% APR on balance transfers for 15 months (3% transfer fee), then 21.9% APR.

Rules:
1. Start by asking about the customer's goal. Ask at most 3 questions before recommending.
2. Recommend at most 2 options. For each, explain WHY it fits their stated goal and the main trade-off (fees, APR, conditions).
3. Never promise approval. Say approval depends on a credit review.
4. Say clearly when you are unsure or when information is missing.
5. Before preparing an application, explain what information you need and why. Ask only for what is necessary.
6. Never submit anything without showing a full summary and getting an explicit "Yes, submit". Make it easy to say no, edit or cancel.
7. Offer to connect the customer with a human banker if they seem unsure, upset, or ask about debt hardship, disputes or complaints.
8. Do not give investment, tax or legal advice.

Greet the customer and ask what they'd like help with.

Concept and usability tasks

Task Scenario Success looks like Autonomy level
T1 Express a need "You're planning a trip abroad. Get help choosing a card." Goal captured in 3 questions or fewer L0–L1
T2 Understand the pick "Explain why Atlas recommended this card." Correct teach-back of rationale and fee L1
T3 Spot the catch "What happens after the 0% period?" Identifies the 21.9% APR and transfer fee L1
T4 Prepare and review "Let Atlas fill in an application." Reviews the summary; notices a planted error L2
T5 Approve or refuse "Decide whether to submit." Makes an informed choice; can decline easily L3
T6 Get a person "You want to talk to someone about your debt." Reaches a banker hand-off Recourse

Three concept variants are compared: Advisor (L0–L1 only), Co-pilot (up to L3) and Autopilot (L4 rules), measuring preference, trust and willingness to delegate for each.

Stakeholder partnership

Research is built with partners from the start, so findings arrive in a form each team can act on.

Partner What they need from research How they're involved

Design Where explanations, consent and controls fail Observe sessions; joint synthesis

Data Science Metrics that link behavior to trust and outcomes Co-define benchmarks and pilot analytics
Compliance, Risk, Legal Evidence that disclosures, consent and recommendations are understood and fair Review instruments and stimuli; responsible-AI review each round

Research operations: a shared session workbook, a qualitative codebook, consent and data-handling standards, and a repeatable readout template.
